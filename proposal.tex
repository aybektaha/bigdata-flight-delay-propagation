\section*{Abstract}
\textbf{Problem Definition:} In commercial air transportation, aircraft operate cyclic daily rotations where an arrival delay on one leg directly threatens the scheduled departure of the next. Standard flight delay studies treat each flight as an independent observation and train basic classifiers for 15-minute delays, ignoring physical aircraft rotations. Conversely, network queuing models in operations research track propagation trees but rely on single-node solvers that collapse under multi-year national flight volumes. We address this scalability bottleneck by implementing an airframe-tracking pipeline in Apache Spark across 31.7 million U.S. domestic flights spanning 2018 through 2022 ($\approx 9.8$ GB uncompressed CSV). Using distributed window functions partitioned by aircraft registrations (\texttt{Tail\_Number}), the pipeline links consecutive flight legs, measures turnaround buffer absorption across major hubs, and benchmarks distributed execution against a single-machine Pandas baseline.

\section{Project Aim and Motivation}
Primary delays originate from localized disruptions like thunderstorms, runway maintenance, or gate-crew shortages. Secondary (reactionary) delays occur when an aircraft lands late and the scheduled ground turnaround time cannot absorb the deficit before the next departure. In dense hub networks, reactionary delays frequently exceed primary delay minutes during afternoon operating banks.

This project focuses on three operational and computational questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What minimum turnaround buffer ($B_{sched}$) is required at congested hubs to absorb arrival delays without pushing back the next departure?
    \item Which hub airports act as systemic delay absorbers versus delay amplifiers during peak traffic hours?
    \item What is the multi-leg propagation multiplier ($R_0$) of an initial morning delay across the remainder of an aircraft's daily rotation?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Why Big Data Technologies Are Required:} Tracking aircraft itineraries across five calendar years requires sorting and stateful windowing over 31.7 million records. A commercial airliner flies between 4 and 8 legs per day. Chaining these legs requires an expensive distributed window operation:
\begin{center}
\texttt{Window.partitionBy("Tail\_Number").orderBy("FlightDate", "CRSDepTime")}
\end{center}
In a single-machine Pandas environment with 16 GB RAM, this operation triggers severe out-of-memory (OOM) failures; the high-cardinality grouping and in-memory sorting saturate RAM and force OS swap thrashing. PySpark solves this by hash-partitioning aircraft across worker cores, spilling intermediate shuffles to disk when partition buffers fill, and applying Catalyst column pruning before physical execution.

\section{Data}
We analyze the \textit{Flight Delay Dataset 2018--2022}, compiled by Rob Mulla from official U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) Form 41 reporting, hosted on Kaggle (\url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/robikscube/flight-delay-dataset-20182022}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type and Volume:} Five annual CSV files (\texttt{Combined\_Flights\_2018.csv} through \texttt{2022.csv}) comprising \textbf{9.8 GB of raw tabular data} (31,746,841 rows and 61 columns), well above the 1 GB course threshold. The compressed Parquet distribution totals 1.06 GB.
    \item \textbf{Core Features:} Aircraft identifiers (\texttt{Tail\_Number}, \texttt{Operating\_Airline}), route endpoints (\texttt{Origin}, \texttt{Dest}), local timestamps (\texttt{CRSDepTime}, \texttt{DepTime}, \texttt{CRSArrTime}, \texttt{ArrTime}), delay minutes (\texttt{DepDelay}, \texttt{ArrDelay}), and BTS delay causes (\texttt{LateAircraftDelay}, \texttt{CarrierDelay}, \texttt{WeatherDelay}, \texttt{NASDelay}).
    \item \textbf{Chunking and Partitioning Strategy:} Raw CSVs will be converted to columnar Parquet partitioned on disk by \texttt{Year} and \texttt{Month}. For sequence linking, Spark DataFrames will be explicitly repartitioned via \texttt{hash(Tail\_Number)}. This guarantees that an aircraft's entire chronological flight history resides within a single executor partition, eliminating redundant network shuffle traffic during lag evaluations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Tools and Frameworks}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Apache Spark (PySpark):} Primary engine for distributed ingestion, hash repartitioning, and chronological window transformations across multi-core executors.
    \item \textbf{Apache Parquet:} Columnar format with Snappy compression, reducing disk footprint by 88\% and enabling pushdown predicates on \texttt{Origin} and \texttt{FlightDate} so queries only read needed columns.
    \item \textbf{Pandas / DuckDB Baseline:} Serves as a single-node control benchmark on 500 MB, 2 GB, and 5 GB slices to empirically identify the data threshold where single-machine memory architectures crash.
    \item \textbf{Plotly / Matplotlib:} Used for generating buffer absorption curves, hub propagation heatmaps, and scalability benchmark profiles.
\end{itemize}

\section{Expected Outcomes and Evaluation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scalability Benchmarks:} Empirical performance comparison between single-node Pandas and distributed PySpark on datasets scaling from 500 MB to 9.8 GB, reporting elapsed execution time, peak JVM heap memory, and core speed-up ratios.
    \item \textbf{Buffer Absorption Thresholds:} Quantification of net delay variation $\Delta D = \text{DepDelay}_{k+1} - \text{ArrDelay}_k$ against scheduled buffer $B_{sched} = \text{CRSDepTime}_{k+1} - \text{CRSArrTime}_k$, identifying the critical minutes required for 80\% delay absorption.
    \item \textbf{Hub Resilience Taxonomy:} Classification of major airports (ATL, ORD, DFW, DEN, JFK) into delay-absorbing versus delay-amplifying nodes, controlling for seasonal weather and peak departure banks.
    \item \textbf{Documented Codebase and Heuristics:} A reproducible PySpark repository and operational rules indicating where schedule padding delivers the highest downstream delay reduction.
\end{enumerate}
